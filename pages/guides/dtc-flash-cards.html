Spoken answer

Because this is a corporate issue.

For your information only: It is not an ABS (an asset-backed security backed by a pool of receivables, such as auto loans or credit-card receivables) or a CMO (a collateralized mortgage obligation, which is a mortgage-backed security split into tranches).

Layman’s terms private — do not read aloud

DTC sorts issues by type. “Corporate” means a company-style instrument. An ABS is backed by a pool of receivables, such as auto loans or credit-card receivables. It is not a pool of art or jewels. A CMO is a mortgage-backed security split into tranches. This file is marked corporate, not those other buckets.

Security Classification Types — Comprehensive Analysis

Corporate Securities: Corporate securities represent ownership or debt in a corporation. These include common stock, preferred stock, corporate bonds, and commercial paper. They are issued by corporations to raise capital for business operations, expansion, or other corporate purposes. Corporate securities are subject to SEC registration unless exempt under rules like Regulation D or Rule 144A.

Asset-Backed Securities (ABS): ABS are financial securities backed by a pool of assets, typically illiquid assets like loans, leases, credit card debt, auto loans, or receivables. The assets serve as collateral for the security. Common types include mortgage-backed securities (though often categorized separately), auto loan ABS, credit card ABS, and student loan ABS. ABS allow financial institutions to convert illiquid assets into marketable securities.

Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO): CMOs are a type of mortgage-backed security that contains a pool of mortgages bundled together and sold as an investment. They are structured into multiple tranches with varying risk profiles, maturities, and interest rates. CMOs are designed to redistribute the prepayment risk and interest rate risk among different investor classes. The underlying collateral is typically residential or commercial mortgages.

DTC Classification Impact: DTC's classification system affects:

  • Eligibility requirements and documentation
  • Settlement processes and timing
  • Fee structures and participant requirements
  • Regulatory reporting obligations

Regulatory Considerations: Each security type has different regulatory frameworks. Corporate securities fall under standard securities regulations, while ABS and CMO may have additional requirements under securities acts, banking regulations, and consumer protection laws depending on the underlying assets and issuer structure.

Card 6

Can you explain to me why you chose Article 8?

Spoken answer

New York Uniform Commercial Code Article 8 is the statute for investment securities. Section 8-102 defines a security. Section 8-103 classifies certain obligations and interests as securities or financial assets.

Those sections do not require a CUSIP. A security may be certificated or uncertificated, and it may be in registered or bearer form.

For your information only: CUSIP Global Services expands CUSIP as Committee on Uniform Security Identification Procedures. The SEC and the Treasury use the plural, Securities.

Layman’s terms private — do not read aloud

Think of Article 8 as New York’s rulebook for investment securities — how they are recognized and transferred. A CUSIP is the industry ID for an issue. It is not one of the tests in Sections 8-102 and 8-103.

Article 8 of the UCC — Comprehensive Analysis

Historical Context: Article 8 of the Uniform Commercial Code was adopted by all 50 states to modernize the law governing investment securities. It replaced the outdated Uniform Negotiable Instruments Law and provided a framework for electronic securities transfers.

Core Definitions: Article 8 defines what constitutes a "security" and establishes rules for certificated and uncertificated securities. It distinguishes between registered and bearer securities, and provides rules for their transfer and perfection of security interests.

Key Sections: Sections 102 and 103 specifically address the definition of securities and their classification. These sections are fundamental to determining whether an instrument qualifies for DTC eligibility and book-entry settlement.

State Adoption Variations: While Article 8 is uniform across states, some states have adopted different versions (pre-1994, 1994, or 2002 revisions). The differences primarily affect the treatment of uncertificated securities and control concepts.

DTC Relevance: DTC eligibility practice asks for a CUSIP and for the securities to be held in a form DTC can process. That is DTC's operational requirement. It is not the text of Sections 8-102 and 8-103.

Practical Impact: Article 8 covers both certificated and uncertificated securities. A CUSIP helps the market identify the issue. It is not an element of the statutory definition of a security.

Card 8

Can you tell me why you checked “Eligible for Resale under Rule 144A”?

Spoken answer

Rule 144A is a “safe harbor” exemption from the ’33 Act’s registration requirements for resale of certain restricted securities to qualified institutional buyers (QIBs).

Layman’s terms private — do not read aloud

The 1933 Securities Act normally says: register with the SEC before you sell to the public. A “safe harbor” is a written path that keeps you inside an exemption if you follow its rules. Rule 144A is that path for selling restricted paper only to QIBs (Qualified Institutional Buyers) — large professional institutions, not the general public — without a full public registration.

Rule 144A — Comprehensive Analysis

Legal Definition: Rule 144A (U.S. Securities Act of 1933, Sections 5 and 17 CFR § 230.144A) allows for the immediate resale of private placement securities among Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) without requiring public registration. It provides a safe harbor from the registration requirements of the Securities Act of 1933, facilitating liquidity in the institutional market.

Historical Context: Rule 144A was adopted by the SEC in 1990 to facilitate the development of a more efficient private placement market for institutional investors. It was designed to address the liquidity problems faced by restricted securities.

QIB Definition: A Qualified Institutional Buyer (QIB) is an entity that owns and invests at least $100 million in securities. This includes banks, insurance companies, registered investment companies, and certain other financial institutions.

Key Requirements: To qualify for Rule 144A, securities must be sold only to QIBs, and the seller must take reasonable steps to ensure the buyer is a QIB. The securities must be of the same class as those registered under the Securities Act.

Market Impact: Rule 144A has become the principal method for foreign companies to access U.S. capital markets without full SEC registration. It facilitates liquidity in the secondary market for restricted securities among institutional investors.

Operational Benefits: For DTC eligibility, Rule 144A designation allows for faster settlement and reduced documentation requirements compared to traditional registration methods. It's particularly valuable for foreign issuers seeking U.S. investor access.

Regulatory Framework: Rule 144A works in conjunction with Regulation S (for foreign offerings) and Rule 144 (for general restricted securities). Together, these rules create a comprehensive framework for private placement markets.

Card 9

Can you tell me why you checked “Eligible for Resale under Regulation S of the ’33 Act”?

Spoken answer

Regulation S is a “safe harbor” exemption from the ’33 Act’s registration requirements for resale of certain restricted securities to “foreign” qualified institutional buyers (QIBs) that may not resell the issue in the United States or to U.S. corporations abroad.

Layman’s terms private — do not read aloud

Regulation S is the offshore companion to 144A. In everyday language: sell outside the United States, do not market into the U.S., and the foreign buyer is not supposed to turn around and dump the issue back into the U.S. market or to U.S. companies abroad.

Regulation S — Comprehensive Analysis

Legal Definition: Regulation S (U.S. Securities Act of 1933, Sections 5 and 17 CFR Part 230) is a safe harbor that defines when an offering of securities will be deemed to come to rest abroad and therefore not be subject to the registration obligations imposed under Section 5 of the 1933 Act. It demands that offers and sales be made outside the United States and that no offering participant engage in directed selling efforts.

Purpose and Framework: Regulation S is a "safe harbor" that defines when an offering of securities will be deemed to come to rest abroad and therefore not be subject to the registration obligations imposed under Section 5 of the 1933 Act. It includes two safe harbor provisions: an issuer safe harbor and a resale safe harbor.

Key Requirements: The regulation demands that offers and sales of securities be made outside the United States and that no offering participant (including the issuer, banks assisting with the offer, and their affiliates) engage in "directed selling efforts."

Directed Selling Efforts: For issuers whose securities have substantial U.S. market interest, the regulation also requires that no offers and sales be made to U.S. persons (including U.S. persons physically located outside the United States).

Historical Context: Section 5 of the 1933 Act is meant primarily as protection for United States investors. Historically, the SEC had only weakly enforced registration of foreign transactions and had limited constitutional authority to do so. Regulation S provides clearer guidelines for international offerings.

Practical Impact: Regulation S has become essential for non-U.S. companies seeking to raise capital while avoiding the costs and requirements of full U.S. registration. It works in tandem with Rule 144A to provide comprehensive offshore access to U.S. institutional investors.

Issuer Name & Issue Description — Comprehensive Analysis

Issuer Name Definition: The issuer name is the legal entity that creates and sells the security. This is the party obligated to make payments (interest, dividends, principal) to security holders. For corporate securities, this is typically the corporation itself. For government securities, it's the government entity issuing the debt.

Issue Description Definition: The issue description identifies the specific security being offered through a textual description. This includes details such as the security type (bond, stock, note), maturity date, interest rate, and other distinguishing features. This is different from the CUSIP number, which is a unique identifier code.

CUSIP Identification System: The CUSIP (Committee on Uniform Security Identification Procedures) number is a nine-character alphanumeric identifier that uniquely identifies a specific security. The first six characters identify the issuer, characters 7-8 identify the specific issue, and the ninth is a check digit for validation.

DTC Requirements: For DTC eligibility, the issuer name and issue description must:

  • Match exactly the issuer's legal name on the Certificate of Incorporation or charter
  • Correspond to the CUSIP assigned to the specific issue
  • Be consistent across all documentation (prospectus, offering documents, registration statements)
  • Reflect the exact security type and terms being offered

Regulatory Importance: Accurate issuer and issue identification is critical for:

  • SEC registration and compliance requirements
  • Tax reporting and withholding obligations
  • Corporate action processing (dividends, splits, tenders)
  • Settlement and clearance through depositories

Documentation Consistency: The issuer name and issue description must be consistent across all documents submitted to DTC, including the eligibility questionnaire,CUSIP information page, and supporting documentation. Inconsistencies can delay or prevent eligibility approval.

Yes. It is a book-entry-only issue recorded electronically, meaning trading is handled entirely through depository book-entry entries rather than moving physical certificates. While a single global certificate is held by the depository, ownership tracking and settlement occur electronically.

However, please note that operational rules differ if an investor withdraws shares to interact directly with the transfer agent for physical issuance or direct registration.

Book-Entry-Only Systems — Comprehensive Analysis

Historical Evolution: Book-entry systems represent a fundamental shift from physical certificate-based securities settlement to electronic record-keeping. The move accelerated in the 1960s-1970s due to the "paperwork crisis" in securities markets, where physical certificate volumes overwhelmed processing capabilities. The Depository Trust Company (DTC) was established in 1973 specifically to address this crisis.

Operational Mechanics: In book-entry systems, ownership is recorded electronically on the books of depositories, transfer agents, and intermediaries. When securities are traded, no physical certificates move between parties. Instead, electronic entries are made in the accounts of the buyer and seller, with the depository maintaining the ultimate ownership record.

Benefits: Book-entry systems provide:

  • Elimination of certificate storage and handling costs
  • Reduced settlement times (from T+5 to T+2 or T+1)
  • Lower risk of certificate loss, theft, or forgery
  • Increased market liquidity and trading efficiency
  • Automated corporate action processing

DTC Book-Entry System: DTC's book-entry system is the backbone of U.S. securities settlement. It maintains ownership records for trillions of dollars of securities. Participants hold securities in "street name" (their own name) on behalf of beneficial owners, creating a chain of electronic ownership from DTC through brokers to individual investors.

Global Certificate Connection: Many book-entry-only securities are backed by a single global certificate held by DTC. This certificate represents the entire issue and is never delivered to individual holders, ensuring all trades remain electronic while maintaining the legal framework of certificated securities.

International Standard: Book-entry systems have become the global standard for securities settlement. Major markets worldwide have adopted electronic settlement systems, and cross-border initiatives like the European Central Securities Depositories (ECSD) network further integrate global electronic settlement.

Put and Tender Features — Comprehensive Analysis

Put Options: A put option gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to sell the security back to the issuer at a predetermined price (the strike price) within a specified time period. Put features in fixed income securities are often designed to protect investors against interest rate risk, allowing them to redeem the security if rates rise and they can reinvest at higher rates.

Tender Offers: A tender offer is when the issuer invites holders to submit their securities for purchase at a specified price, usually at a premium to market price. Tender features are commonly used in preferred stock and certain debt instruments to provide liquidity to holders and give issuers flexibility in managing their capital structure.

QIB Attractiveness: Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) value put/tender features because they:

  • Provide downside protection and liquidity
  • Allow for portfolio rebalancing without market impact
  • Reduce holding period uncertainty
  • Enable active risk management strategies

DTC Requirements: For securities with put/tender features, DTC requires specific documentation:

  • Tender Letter of Representations
  • Clear procedures for tender offer processing
  • Defined settlement timelines for tender events
  • Notification requirements for upcoming tender periods

Market Impact: Put/tender features can significantly affect securities pricing and trading behavior. Securities with these features often trade at premiums compared to similar securities without such features, reflecting the embedded option value. The presence of these features can also influence secondary market liquidity and trading patterns.

Regulatory Considerations: Put and tender features must comply with SEC regulations, particularly regarding disclosure requirements, tender offer procedures under Rule 14e-1, and fair dealing provisions. Issuers must ensure that tender features are properly disclosed in offering documents and that tender processes are conducted fairly and transparently.

Transfer Agents — Comprehensive Analysis

Core Functions: Transfer agents are financial institutions, typically banks or trust companies, appointed by issuers to maintain official records of security ownership. Their primary responsibilities include maintaining the shareholder register, processing transfers of ownership, handling corporate actions, and distributing dividends and interest payments.

Regulatory Oversight: Transfer agents are regulated by the SEC under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and must register with the SEC. They are subject to rigorous compliance requirements, including anti-money laundering (AML) regulations, Know Your Customer (KYC) procedures, and regular audits to ensure accuracy and security of ownership records.

DTC Integration: Transfer agents work closely with DTC and other depositories to facilitate electronic settlement. They maintain book-entry records, process FAST (Fast Automated Securities Transfer) transactions, and ensure that ownership changes are accurately reflected in both their records and DTC's systems.

Corporate Actions: Transfer agents play a critical role in corporate actions such as:

  • Stock splits and reverse splits
  • Mergers and acquisitions
  • Dividend and interest distributions
  • Proxy voting coordination
  • Right offerings and subscription warrants

Market Infrastructure: Major transfer agents include institutions like Computershare, Equiniti, American Stock Transfer & Trust Company, and large banks like Bank of America, JPMorgan Chase, and Wells Fargo. These institutions handle trillions of dollars in securities for thousands of issuers.

Technology and Security: Modern transfer agents use sophisticated electronic systems to maintain ownership records, with real-time synchronization to depositories and market participants. Security measures include encryption, multi-factor authentication, and redundant systems to ensure data integrity and business continuity.

Fee Structure: Transfer agents charge issuers fees based on the number of registered holders, transaction volume, and services provided. These costs are typically borne by the issuer as part of the ongoing expenses of maintaining publicly traded securities.

F.A.S.T. System — Comprehensive Analysis

Definition and Purpose: FAST (Fast Automated Securities Transfer) is DTC's electronic system that enables transfer agents to electronically provide custody, transfer, deposit, and withdrawal services. It eliminates the need for physical movement of securities certificates.

Operational Process: When a security is transferred to a QIB, the transfer agent sends an electronic instruction to DTC. DTC then records the transfer on its books, updating the beneficial ownership without any physical certificate movement.

DTC Eligibility: For DTC eligibility under FAST, issuers must execute a FAST Letter of Representations and ensure their transfer agent is a FAST participant. This facilitates seamless electronic transfers between DTC participants.

Industry Impact: FAST has dramatically reduced settlement times from T+3 to same-day for many transactions, improved operational efficiency, and reduced costs associated with physical certificate handling. It's a cornerstone of modern securities settlement infrastructure.

What does UNCITRAL mean?

Spoken answer

Layman’s terms private — do not read aloud

UNCITRAL — Comprehensive Analysis

Historical Context: UNCITRAL (United Nations Commission on International Trade Law) was established by the UN General Assembly in 1966 to address the growing need for harmonization and unification of international trade law. Prior to UNCITRAL, international trade was governed by disparate national laws, creating legal uncertainty and increased transaction costs.

Core Mission: UNCITRAL's primary mission is to promote the progressive harmonization and unification of international trade law through the preparation and promotion of legislative and non-legislative texts in key areas of commercial law. The organization works to reduce legal obstacles to international trade while ensuring equitable treatment for all parties.

Key Legislative Texts: UNCITRAL has produced numerous influential legal instruments including:

  • United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG): Governs contracts for the sale of goods between parties in different countries
  • UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration: Provides a framework for international arbitration proceedings
  • United Nations Convention on International Bills of Exchange and International Promissory Notes: Harmonizes rules for negotiable instruments
  • UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce: Addresses legal recognition of electronic communications and signatures

Securities Relevance: UNCITRAL's work directly impacts securities markets through:

  • Model Law on Secured Transactions - provides legal framework for security interests in movable property
  • Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration - used in cross-border securities disputes
  • Electronic commerce frameworks supporting electronic securities settlement
  • Harmonized rules for cross-border insolvency affecting securities holders

Global Adoption: UNCITRAL model laws have been adopted by over 80 countries, creating significant legal harmonization in international trade. The CISG alone has been ratified by 95 countries, making it one of the most successful international commercial law instruments.

Impact on DTC Operations: For DTC and other depositories, UNCITRAL harmonization reduces legal complexity in cross-border securities transactions, provides predictable legal frameworks for international settlements, and supports the growth of global securities markets through standardized legal principles.

Card 101

What does CUSIP mean?

Spoken answer

CUSIP is an acronym for Committee on Uniform Security Identification Procedures, and the nine-digit alphanumeric CUSIP numbers used to identify securities.

Layman’s terms private — do not read aloud

The barcode / serial number for a specific stock or bond. Nine characters. Assigned so every issue has its own ID in the clearing system.

CUSIP System — Comprehensive Analysis

Structure and Format: CUSIP identifiers are 9-character alphanumeric codes. The first six characters (CUSIP-6) uniquely identify the issuer. Issuer codes are assigned alphabetically with deliberate built-in "gaps" for future expansion. The 7th and 8th digits identify the exact issue, and the 9th digit is an automatically generated checksum.

Issue Identification: The 7th and 8th digit format depends on security type. Numbers are typically used for equities, letters for fixed income. For commercial paper, the first issue character is generated from the letter code of the maturity month, the second from the day of maturity (using letters for numbers over 9).

Check Digit Calculation: The 9th digit uses the "Modulus 10 Double Add Double" technique. Every second digit is multiplied by two. Letters are converted to numbers by adding their ordinal position in the alphabet to 9 (A=10, M=22). The resulting digits are summed, and the ten's complement of the last number becomes the check digit.

Calculation Examples:

  • Apple Inc (037833100): Check digit calculated as (0+7+3+1) + (6+16+6+0) = 30. Ten's complement of 0 is 0.
  • Wal-Mart (931142103): Check digit calculation yields 27. Ten's complement of 7 is 3.
  • US Treasury Note (912827XN7): Letters X=33, N=23. Check digit calculation yields 43. Ten's complement of 3 is 7.

Special Characters: For private placements, the characters *, @, and # may be used (Private Placement Number, PPN). Their numeric values are *=36, @=37, and #=38 for check digit calculation.

International Extension: CINS (CUSIP International Numbering System) adds a single country code letter to the beginning of an otherwise similar CUSIP for international securities, though ISIN has become the more widely used international standard.

  • Funds are transferred through the banking system
  • Settlement occurs on a net basis for efficiency
  • Final settlement is simultaneous, ensuring DVP principles

Book-Entry-Only Systems — Comprehensive Analysis

Historical Evolution: Book-entry systems represent a fundamental shift from physical certificate-based securities settlement to electronic record-keeping. The move accelerated in the 1960s-1970s due to the "paperwork crisis" in securities markets, where physical certificate volumes overwhelmed processing capabilities. The Depository Trust Company (DTC) was established in 1973 specifically to address this crisis.

Operational Mechanics: In book-entry systems, ownership is recorded electronically on the books of depositories, transfer agents, and intermediaries. When securities are traded, no physical certificates move between parties. Instead, electronic entries are made in the accounts of the buyer and seller, with the depository maintaining the ultimate ownership record.

Benefits: Book-entry systems provide:

  • Elimination of certificate storage and handling costs
  • Reduced settlement times (from T+5 to T+2 or T+1)
  • Lower risk of certificate loss, theft, or forgery
  • Increased market liquidity and trading efficiency
  • Automated corporate action processing

DTC Book-Entry System: DTC's book-entry system is the backbone of U.S. securities settlement. It maintains ownership records for trillions of dollars of securities. Participants hold securities in "street name" (their own name) on behalf of beneficial owners, creating a chain of electronic ownership from DTC through brokers to individual investors.

Global Certificate Connection: Many book-entry-only securities are backed by a single global certificate held by DTC. International Standard: Book-entry systems have become the global standard for securities settlement. CUSIP System — Comprehensive Analysis

Structure and Format: CUSIP identifiers are 9-character alphanumeric codes. The first six characters (CUSIP-6) uniquely identify the issuer. Issuer codes are assigned alphabetically with deliberate built-in "gaps" for future expansion. The 7th and 8th digits identify the exact issue, and the 9th digit is an automatically generated checksum.

Issue Identification: The 7th and 8th digit format depends on security type. Numbers are typically used for equities, letters for fixed income. For commercial paper, the first issue character is generated from the letter code of the maturity month, the second from the day of maturity (using letters for numbers over 9).

Check Digit Calculation: The 9th digit uses the "Modulus 10 Double Add Double" technique. Every second digit is multiplied by two. Letters are converted to numbers by adding their ordinal position in the alphabet to 9 (A=10, M=22). The resulting digits are summed, and the ten's complement of the last number becomes the check digit.

Calculation Examples:

  • Apple Inc (037833100): Check digit calculated as (0+7+3+1) + (6+16+6+0) = 30. Ten's complement of 0 is 0.
  • Wal-Mart (931142103): Check digit calculation yields 27. Ten's complement of 7 is 3.
  • US Treasury Note (912827XN7): Letters X=33, N=23. Check digit calculation yields 43. Ten's complement of 3 is 7.

Special Characters: For private placements, the characters *, @, and # may be used (Private Placement Number, PPN). Their numeric values are *=36, @=37, and #=38 for check digit calculation.

International Extension: CINS (CUSIP International Numbering System) adds a single country code letter to the beginning of an otherwise similar CUSIP for international securities, though ISIN has become the more widely used international standard.

Ownership Structure: All securities traded through DTC using global certificates are held in electronic form on the books of various intermediaries between the ultimate owner and DTC participants.   • Enhanced Accuracy: Electronic systems reduce human error

Balancing Confirmation System: A key FAST feature that provides daily reconciliation:

  • Opening position balances for each FAST issue
  • Total credits (deposits and transfers in)
  • Total debits (withdrawals and transfers out)
  • Closing position balances
  • Exception reporting for discrepancies

Security and Risk Management: FAST systems incorporate:

  • Multi-level authentication and access controls
  • Encryption of data transmissions
  • Audit trails for all transactions
  • Real-time monitoring and exception handling
  • Business continuity and disaster recovery planning

Regulatory Compliance: FAST participation ensures compliance with:

  • DTC operational requirements and standards
  • SEC regulations for securities settlement
  • Industry best practices for securities processing
  • Anti-money laundering and know-your-customer requirements

Market Impact: FAST has transformed securities processing by enabling T+1 settlement, reducing operational risk, and providing the infrastructure for modern electronic securities markets. It's considered essential for efficient capital market operations.

You have yourself as the underwriter in the package yet, the Clearing DTC Participant delivered your securities, why is that?

Spoken answer

The clearing participant is the DTC member firm whose account settles the trade.

Layman's terms private — do not read aloud

Even though listed as underwriter, the actual delivery occurs through the clearing participant's account. This is standard practice - the underwriter arranges the offering but the clearing participant handles the actual settlement. PAYMENT is the process whereby any Participant making a Delivery Versus Payment (DVP) of Securities through the facilities of the DTC shall provide the Corporation with an instruction specifying the amount of the payment.

Clearing Participant vs Underwriter Roles — Comprehensive Analysis

Underwriter Role: The underwriter is the financial institution that arranges and guarantees the securities offering. Underwriters purchase securities from the issuer and resell them to investors, assuming the risk of unsold securities. They are responsible for pricing the offering, marketing the securities, and ensuring regulatory compliance. The underwriter's role is primarily focused on the offering process itself.

Clearing Participant Role: The clearing participant is the DTC member firm that maintains the account through which the actual settlement occurs. They are responsible for the operational mechanics of delivering securities and processing payments. The clearing participant provides the infrastructure and systems needed to execute the trades and settle the transactions.

Why the Separation Exists: This separation of roles is standard practice because:

  • Operational Efficiency: Specialized firms can focus on their core competencies
  • Risk Management: Different firms handle different types of risk
  • Regulatory Requirements: Some regulations require functional separation
  • Market Structure: Firms may have different capabilities and relationships

Same Firm, Different Functions: Often the same firm may serve both roles:

  • The investment banking division may act as underwriter
  • The broker-dealer division may act as clearing participant
  • Both divisions are part of the same parent company
  • They operate under different regulatory and operational frameworks

Settlement Process: The actual settlement mechanics involve:

  • Underwriter arranges the offering and sells securities to investors
  • Clearing participant's account receives the securities from the issuer
  • Clearing participant delivers securities to buyers' accounts
  • Clearing participant processes payments through DVP (Delivery Versus Payment)
  • All settlement occurs through the clearing participant's DTC account

DVP Settlement Mechanism: Delivery Versus Payment ensures simultaneous transfer of securities and payment:

  • Securities are transferred between clearing participant accounts
  • Funds are transferred through the banking system
  • Settlement is atomic - both transfers complete or neither does
  • This eliminates settlement risk for both parties
  • The clearing participant provides the payment instructions to DTC

Regulatory and Compliance Considerations: This role separation addresses:

  • SEC regulations regarding underwriting activities
  • DTC requirements for settlement operations
  • FINRA rules for broker-dealer conduct
  • Banking regulations for payment processing

Market Infrastructure Impact: This system enables:

  • Efficient capital raising through specialized underwriting
  • Reliable settlement through established clearing relationships
  • Risk mitigation through functional separation
  • Market liquidity through multiple participant options

Card 305

What is a CUSIP number?

Spoken answer

CUSIP stands for Committee on Uniform Securities Identification Procedures. It is a nine-character alphanumeric code that uniquely identifies financial instruments and their issuers in the U.S. and Canada.

Layman's terms private — do not read aloud

Think of a CUSIP as a Social Security number for securities. The first six characters identify the issuer (company or government), the next two identify the specific issue (stock, bond, etc.), and the ninth is a check digit for accuracy. The letters "I" and "O" are never used to avoid confusion with numbers 1 and 0.

CUSIP — Comprehensive Analysis

History and Development: The CUSIP system was created in July 1964 by the American Bankers Association (ABA) under the chairmanship of John L. Gibbons. It was developed to create a uniform securities identification system to improve efficiency in securities trading and settlement. The system became operational in 1967.

Structure of a CUSIP: A CUSIP identifier consists of nine characters:

  • First 6 characters (Issuer): Identify the issuer (corporation, municipality, or government agency). These are assigned based on an alphabetic sequence of issuer names.
  • Next 2 characters (Issue): Identify the specific issue or type of instrument within that issuer. Numbers are typically used for equities, letters for fixed income. The first security issued is typically "10".
  • 9th character (Check digit): A single numeric check digit calculated using the Modulus 10 Double-Add-Double technique (based on the Luhn algorithm) to verify accuracy of the previous 8 characters.

Character Restrictions: The letters "I" and "O" are never used in CUSIP identifiers to avoid confusion with the numbers "1" and "0". Special characters (*, @, #) may be used for private placements.

Administration: CUSIP Global Services (CGS) is the authorized registration authority for the CUSIP standard (ANSI X9.6). CGS is managed on behalf of the American Bankers Association by FactSet Research Systems Inc. The system covers equity, debt, derivatives, and other financial instruments issued in the U.S. and Canada.

Related Identifiers: CGS also provides CINS (CUSIP International Numbering System) for international securities and CUSIP-based ISINs (International Securities Identification Numbers) for global securities identification.

DTC Relevance: CUSIP numbers are essential for DTC eligibility as they provide the unique identifier used throughout the securities lifecycle—from issuance through settlement and corporate actions. All DTC-eligible securities must have a CUSIP number.

What is a Qualified Institutional Buyer (QIB)?

Spoken answer

A QIB is an investing entity owning and investing large amounts of securities on a discretionary basis.

The threshold is at least $100 million of securities not affiliated with the entity.

Layman's terms private — do not read aloud

A Qualified Institutional Buyer is a purchaser of securities that is deemed financially sophisticated and is legally recognized by security market regulators to need less protection from issuers than most public investors. QIBs are eligible to participate in a restricted market known as the "Rule 144A market" that is not available to the public because the issuers of the securities have chosen not to register the securities.

Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) — Comprehensive Analysis

Regulatory Definition: QIBs are defined under Rule 144A as entities that own and invest at least $100 million in securities on a discretionary basis. This threshold ensures that only financially sophisticated institutions with significant market experience participate in the Rule 144A market. The $100 million threshold is periodically adjusted for inflation by the SEC.

Types of QIBs: Qualified Institutional Buyers include:

  • Registered Investment Companies: Mutual funds, closed-end funds, and ETFs with the required asset threshold
  • Insurance Companies: Both life and property/casualty insurers meeting investment criteria
  • Banks and Savings Associations: Must meet net worth and capital requirements
  • Investment Advisers: Those with at least $100 million in discretionary assets under management
  • Broker-Dealers: Must meet net capital requirements and have significant securities holdings
  • Employee Benefit Plans: With at least $100 million in assets

Market Impact: QIBs are crucial to the Rule 144A market because they:

  • Provide liquidity for restricted securities
  • Enable foreign issuers to access U.S. capital markets without full registration
  • Support efficient price discovery for private placements
  • Reduce the cost of capital for issuers by eliminating public registration expenses

Regulatory Rationale: The QIB concept is based on the premise that large institutional investors have the financial sophistication, resources, and market knowledge to evaluate investment opportunities without the full disclosure protections required for public offerings. This regulatory approach balances investor protection with market efficiency.

Verification Requirements: Issuers selling to QIBs must take reasonable steps to verify buyer status, typically through:

  • Review of publicly available information
  • Representations from the buyer or their counsel
  • Review of regulatory filings (Form ADV for investment advisers, etc.)
  • Knowledge of the buyer's business and financial condition

Market Size: The QIB market represents a significant portion of institutional investment activity. By some estimates, QIBs collectively manage over $20 trillion in assets, making them the dominant force in institutional securities markets and essential to the functioning of the Rule 144A private placement market.

What is the Securities Act of 1933?

Spoken answer

The 1933 Act requires that any offer or sale of securities using interstate commerce be registered, unless an exemption exists.

Layman's terms private — do not read aloud

It was the first major federal legislation to regulate the offer and sale of securities, enacted in the aftermath of the stock market crash of 1929. Its two basic objectives are: to require that investors receive significant (or "material") information concerning securities being offered for public sale; and to prohibit deceit, misrepresentations, and other fraud in the sale of securities to the public.

Securities Act of 1933 — Comprehensive Analysis

Historical Context: Congress enacted the Securities Act of 1933 in the aftermath of the stock market crash of 1929 and during the ensuing Great Depression. It was the first major federal legislation to regulate the offer and sale of securities. Prior to that time, regulation of securities was chiefly governed by state "blue sky" laws.

Core Objectives: The 1933 Act has two basic objectives:

  • Require that investors receive significant (or "material") information concerning securities being offered for public sale
  • Prohibit deceit, misrepresentations, and other fraud in the sale of securities to the public

Registration Requirements: The Act requires that any offer or sale of securities using the means and instrumentalities of interstate commerce be registered with the SEC, unless an exemption from registration exists. Registration statements and prospectuses become public shortly after filing with the SEC.

Key Exemptions: Notable exemptions from registration include:

  • Section 3(a)(11): Intrastate offerings to residents of a single state
  • Section 4(1): Transactions by persons who are not issuers, underwriters, or dealers
  • Section 4(2): Private offerings not involving a public offering
  • Section 4(6): Offers and sales made solely to accredited investors

Regulatory Exemptions: Using its rule-making authority, the SEC has created additional exemptions including Regulation A (offerings up to $5 million), Regulation D (Rules 504, 505, 506), Rule 147 (intrastate offerings), and Rule 1001 (state exemption alternatives).

Rule 144 Specifics: Rule 144 permits limited sales of restricted and controlled securities without registration. It imposes restrictions on holding periods, volume limitations, and filing requirements. Notice of resale is required if sales exceed 5,000 shares or $50,000 in any 3-month period.

Civil Liability: Violation of registration requirements can lead to civil liability for issuers and underwriters under Sections 11, 12(a)(1), or 12(a)(2) of the Act. Additional liability may be imposed under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (Rule 10b-5).

What is Rule 144?

Layman's terms private — do not read aloud

What is a Global Certificate?

Spoken answer

A global certificate is a single certificate representing an entire maturity of an issue of securities.

Layman's terms private — do not read aloud

Sometimes referred to as a "jumbo certificate," such certificates are often used in book-entry systems. The issuer issues a global certificate that is then lodged in the facilities of a securities depository or other book-entry agent and kept safely by the agent until maturity. Global Certificates — Comprehensive Analysis

Definition and Purpose: A global certificate is a single certificate, sometimes referred to as a "jumbo certificate," representing an entire maturity of an issue of securities. Such certificates are often used in book-entry systems to facilitate electronic transfer of interests in securities.

Operational Process: The issuer issues a global certificate that is then lodged in the facilities of a securities depository (like DTC) or other book-entry agent and kept safely by the agent until maturity. The securities are available to beneficial owners only in book-entry form, and no individual certificates can be obtained.

Modern Practice Evolution: Modern practice has developed to eliminate both the need for certificates and maintenance of a complete security register by the issuer. There are two general ways this has been accomplished: non-certificated securities and global certificates.

Non-Certificated vs Global: In some jurisdictions like France, issuers can maintain a legal record of their securities electronically (non-certificated). In the U.S., while Article 8 of the UCC permits non-certificated securities, many states still use older versions. Global certificates provide an alternative path by depositing a single certificate with a depository.

Ownership Structure: All securities traded through DTC using global certificates are held in electronic form on the books of various intermediaries between the ultimate owner and DTC participants. Each intermediary holds on behalf of someone beneath them in the chain, with the ultimate owner called the beneficial owner.

Clearing Process: Among brokerages and mutual fund companies, a large amount of mutual fund share transactions take place among intermediaries as opposed to shares being sold and redeemed directly with the transfer agent. Most of these intermediaries clear the shares electronically through the National Securities Clearing Corp. (NSCC), a subsidiary of DTCC.

What are the key legal and regulatory references for DTC eligibility?

Spoken answer

The key references include Rule 144A, Regulation S, DTC Operational Arrangements, DTC Rules, Article 8 of the UCC, the Securities Act of 1933, the JOBS Act of 2012, and the UNCITRAL Convention.

Layman's terms private — do not read aloud

These are the primary legal frameworks and regulatory documents that govern securities eligibility, exemptions from registration, and DTC's operational requirements.

Key Legal and Regulatory References — Comprehensive Analysis

Rule 144A (U.S. Securities Act of 1933, Sections 5 and 17 CFR § 230.144A): Allows for the immediate resale of private placement securities among Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) without requiring public registration. It provides a safe harbor from the registration requirements of the Securities Act of 1933, facilitating liquidity in the institutional market.

Regulation S (U.S. Securities Act of 1933, Sections 5 and 17 CFR Part 230): A safe harbor that defines when an offering of securities will be deemed to come to rest abroad and therefore not be subject to the registration obligations imposed under Section 5 of the 1933 Act. It demands that offers and sales be made outside the United States and that no offering participant engage in directed selling efforts.

DTC Operational Arrangements: The primary document governing DTC eligibility requirements, operational procedures, and the obligations of issuers, agents, and participants. This document is essential for understanding what is required to make securities eligible for DTC services.

DTC Rules: The rules by which DTC operates and which participants must follow. These rules cover eligibility, participation, settlement, and other operational aspects of DTC's services.

Article 8, Sections 102 and 103 (NY UCC): Article 8 of the New York Uniform Commercial Code defines what constitutes a security and classifies certain obligations and interests as securities or financial assets. Sections 102 and 103 specifically address the definition of securities and their classification, which is fundamental to determining whether an instrument qualifies for DTC eligibility.

U.S. Securities Act of 1933: The first major federal legislation to regulate the offer and sale of securities. It requires that any offer or sale of securities using the means and instrumentalities of interstate commerce be registered, unless an exemption from registration exists. Its objectives are to require that investors receive significant information and to prohibit deceit and fraud.

JOBS Act of 2012: The Jumpstart Our Business Startups Act, which includes provisions affecting securities registration and exemptions. It amended securities laws to facilitate capital formation for small businesses, including changes to Regulation A, Rule 506 of Regulation D, and the creation of Regulation A+.

UNCITRAL Convention: The United Nations Commission on International Trade Law provides a framework for international commercial transactions, including conventions on arbitration, electronic commerce, and secured transactions. The UNCITRAL Convention on the Assignment of Receivables in International Trade is particularly relevant to securities financing and cross-border transactions.

Other Laws and Regulations: Additional relevant frameworks may include state corporate laws, banking regulations, tax laws, and international treaties depending on the specific jurisdiction and nature of the securities issue.
